Two rates for one dollar

The bank says 8.40. The bazaar says about 11. Both are true, and that is what makes this autumn so confusing.

Zholdosh A. · 18 Oct 2026 · Issue 242 min read
The official rate is 8.40 som for a dollar. It has been 8.40 since March 2008. At the bazaar this October the street rate is 11 to 12, and in June it was about 9.5. The difference is the price of the dollar you can actually get.

For a family who earns and spends in som, almost nothing changes: bread is 3.50, plov 25 to 30, petrol 5.40. The difference arrives when you pay for something in dollars: a rent quoted in dollars, a part for the car, a university abroad. Then your som buys 25% less, and the landlord and the shop know exactly how much.

I have two suggestions, no more. Ask which rate any dollar price uses before you agree to it. And write the price in som on the receipt, so that later nobody remembers it differently. When the rates come closer, and they will, you will want that paper.

Living with the $300 limit

Talasbank now pays out at most $300 of your dollars a month. Eleven things families are doing, honestly assessed.

Zholdosh A. · 31 May 2026 · Issue 212 min read
Since the spring the dollar cash at the banks has been short, and this month Talasbank put a cap: $300 a month per customer. Here is what I hear in the queue and on the telephone.
  • Paying rent in som. The landlord grumbles but accepts. It works if the landlord is also tired of queuing.
  • Pooling the family limit. Each adult takes his $300, one person holds the pot. Works. Needs trust.
  • Asking relatives abroad to send in som. Works, but the transfer office takes a share.
  • Buying at the bazaar rate. It is more expensive. I do not recommend or condemn, I only say the rate is not the bank's.
  • Postponing the car. Wise.
  • Postponing the wedding. Not wise, but understood.
  • Barter among neighbours. Older than the dollar.
I am not able to tell you which is the best, because it depends on your family. I can tell you which is the worst: panic. Panic buys at the worst rate on the worst day.

A pension, a grandson and a phone

Pensioners in Irad are the most reliable savers in the country and the least rewarded for it.

Zholdosh A. · 21 Apr 2024 · Issue 172 min read
The average pension is about 1,200 som a month. Out of this a pensioner buys bread, medicine, electricity, a bus ticket, and a gift for each grandchild's birthday. The last one is not optional. It is paid, as it was paid to her, in love.

I visited an old couple in Talas who live on two pensions, 2,350 together. They told me what changed in ten years. The medicine got more expensive. The bread stayed the same. The grandson who used to ask for sweets now asks for "credit" on his phone. They pay it every month, 20 som. The grandmother says it is the best money she spends, because on the phone she hears his voice and not just his request.

What would help, I asked? They thought for a long time. "A bench near the pharmacy," the grandfather said. "To sit between the queue and the bus."

The remittance queue

Money from relatives abroad still holds up a lot of Irad's families. A Saturday at a transfer office.

Zholdosh A. · 11 Feb 2024 · Issue 162 min read
On Saturday mornings the transfer office near the bazaar has a queue out of the door. Mostly women, mostly with a telephone in one hand and a passport in the other, waiting for the code from a son or a husband in Russia or Kazakhstan.

The amounts are small and regular. Rent, school, a debt at the shop, a part for the car. What these transfers buy is not luxury, it is time: a family with a regular transfer can wait for a better price, a better job, a better season. A family without one cannot wait for anything.

Reading a microloan slowly

An office on every high street will lend you 3,000 som by tomorrow. Here is how to read the paper they give you.

Zholdosh A. · 14 Aug 2022 · Issue 112 min read
I took a typical offer from a microfinance office in Talas (not naming it, not accusing it, just reading it). Loan 3,000 som for 12 months. "Interest 2.4% a month". Sounds small. Over a year that is nearly 29% on the amount, but because you repay a part each month and the interest is counted on the whole amount, the real yearly cost is closer to 50%.

Then three extra lines: an "insurance fee" (60 som), a "processing fee" (45), and a "late payment" penalty of 1% a day. If you are two weeks late, the penalty alone is the price of a pair of winter boots.

The microloan is not a trap. For a woman buying a sewing machine or a man buying seed, it can be the difference between a business and no business. But it is a tool, not a present. Before you sign: ask for the total you will pay back, in som, on one line. If they cannot write it, do not sign.

The sheep you feed for six months

Is it cheaper to buy mutton at the bazaar, or to keep one sheep for the winter? I asked a family in the village of Kyzyl-Adyr to show me their notebook.

Zholdosh A. · 12 Dec 2021 · Issue 72 min read
The sheep was bought in June at 380 som. Hay and barley for six months: 290. The share of the neighbour's boy who took it out to graze with his own: 40. A visit from the vet: 15. In December the sheep weighed about 52 kilos on the hoof and produced 24 kilos of meat, plus fat, plus the skin.

At the bazaar price of mutton, 24 kilos of meat is worth about 840 som. So the family "earned" 115 som on top of what they spent, plus the fat. But they also spent the whole summer and autumn: the walk every evening, the cutting of hay, the winter fear that it would fall sick. If a family counted its time at the minimum wage, the sheep lost money.

They told me: "You don't understand. The sheep is not meat, it is the thing you can offer to a guest." And that is the other half of the arithmetic.

What a toi really costs

I added up eleven weddings in Talas and the villages. The hall is not the expensive part.

Zholdosh A. · 14 Nov 2021 · Issue 62 min read
Families tell me the wedding hall is the big cost. In the eleven budgets I collected it was on average less than a fifth. The real money goes on meat, on the gifts both families exchange, on the clothes, and on the second and third smaller feasts that nobody counts as "the wedding" but everybody attends.

The gifts are the interesting part, because they are not really spending: they are a savings scheme that runs over generations. What you give at your neighbour's son's toi comes back, more or less, at your daughter's. The problem starts when a family stops being invited and keeps giving.
